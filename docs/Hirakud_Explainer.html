Executive Summary (Read This First)

Hirakud Dam sits on the Mahanadi River in Odisha. Every day during the monsoon, the people who operate it must decide how much water to let out. To decide well, they need to know how much water is about to arrive — days before it does.

This project builds a system that answers that question for the next 1 to 17 days. It works in two stages, and each stage uses a different kind of artificial-intelligence model, taken from a different published research paper.

StageThe problem it solvesThe tool
Stage 1 The global weather forecast is far too blurry. One of its grid squares is about 166 km across — bigger than the distance from Delhi to Agra — and it gives just one rainfall number for that whole area. A convolutional neural network, the kind of AI used for recognising objects in photographs. It sharpens the blurry forecast into a detailed one.
Stage 2 Rainfall is not the same thing as river flow. Some rain soaks in, some evaporates, some runs off — and it arrives at the dam days later. A transformer, the same family of model behind ChatGPT, but adapted to read a future rainfall sequence and output a flow forecast.

What we found

  • Sharpening the forecast works. The AI-sharpened rainfall is measurably better than the raw global forecast, which on its own has essentially no local skill at all.
  • If we tell the second model exactly how much rain actually fell, it predicts inflow very well — and remarkably, it gets better at longer ranges, not worse.
  • When we instead give it a real forecast of rain, performance falls sharply.
The single most important conclusion That drop is not caused by a weakness in our river model. It is caused by the rainfall forecast itself being wrong. This matters enormously, because it means the way to improve this system is better weather information, not a cleverer river model. We did not just assume this — we built a full physics-based river model to test it, and the physics-based model did worse when fed forecast rainfall, not better.

The honest bottom line: the system is genuinely useful out to about 7 days. Between 8 and 17 days it is still slightly better than simply guessing the seasonal average, but only slightly, and this document says so plainly rather than hiding it.

1   The Problem: Why Anyone Needs This

1.1 What is a dam, in this context?

A dam is a wall across a river. Behind it, water collects in an artificial lake called a reservoir. Hirakud Dam holds back the Mahanadi River in Odisha, and the water it stores is used for three things: preventing floods downstream, irrigating farmland, and generating electricity.

Two words are used constantly in this document:

Definitions Inflow — the water arriving into the reservoir from the river and the land upstream. This is the thing we are trying to predict.

Catchment (or basin) — the whole area of land that drains into the dam. When rain falls anywhere in this area, some of it eventually reaches the reservoir. Hirakud's catchment is about 85,000 km², roughly the size of Austria.

Cumec — the unit of river flow: one cubic metre of water per second. A large flood at Hirakud can exceed 20,000 cumecs, which is 20,000 tonnes of water passing every second.

1.2 The operator's dilemma

Here is the actual human problem, and it is worth stating before any mathematics.

A dam operator must release water before they know how much is coming.

  • Release too little, and the reservoir fills up. When the flood finally arrives there is nowhere to put it, so you must dump enormous volumes suddenly — which floods the towns downstream. The dam has made things worse, not better.
  • Release too much, and you have thrown away water that farmers needed for irrigation and that the turbines needed for power.

Once the water has arrived, it is too late to optimise. The decision had to be made days earlier. That is why forecasting matters: it converts an impossible decision into a merely difficult one.

1.3 Why can't we just forecast the rain and be done?

Because the same rainfall produces wildly different amounts of river flow, depending on how wet the ground already was.

Analogy — the sponge Ask: “if I pour one litre of water on this sponge, will it drip?” The honest answer is “it depends what you poured on it last week.” A bone-dry sponge soaks up everything and drips nothing. A sponge already saturated drips almost the whole litre immediately.

Soil behaves exactly the same way. 100 mm of rain on dry soil at the start of the monsoon mostly soaks in. The same 100 mm falling on soil already saturated from a week of rain becomes almost pure runoff, and races into the reservoir.

So any useful model must know not only the rainfall forecast, but also how wet the catchment already is. This is called the antecedent condition — simply, "the state of things beforehand." Every design decision in Stage 2 follows from this one fact.

1.4 Why can't we use the global weather forecast directly?

Because it is far too coarse. This is explained fully in Section 2, but the short version is that the global model divides the world into squares about 166 km wide and reports a single rainfall number for each. One such square covers the uplands of Chhattisgarh, dense forest, and the Mahanadi valley floor all at once, averaged into one number. The differences between those places — which are exactly what determines where water runs off — are invisible.

1.5 What “sub-seasonal” means, and why it is the hardest window

Forecasting problems fall along a spectrum of how far ahead you are looking:

   Hours - 3 days    3 - 10 days      10 - 17 days         3+ months
  +--------------+ +--------------+ +----------------+ +---------------+
  |   WEATHER    | | MEDIUM-RANGE | |  SUB-SEASONAL  | |    CLIMATE    |
  |              | |              | | (THIS PROJECT) | |               |
  +--------------+ +--------------+ +----------------+ +---------------+
   Very reliable    Fairly good     *** THE GAP ***    Broad averages
                                                        only

Why is the middle window the hard one? There are two completely different reasons forecasts can work, and sub-seasonal falls between them.

  1. Short-range forecasts work because of memory. Today's atmosphere strongly determines tomorrow's. Measure it accurately now, run the physics forward, and you get a good answer. But this memory fades — after roughly one to two weeks, tiny errors in today's measurement have grown large enough to swamp the forecast.
  2. Seasonal forecasts work for a different reason. Slow, heavy things — ocean temperatures, soil moisture — nudge the average weather over months. They cannot tell you about next Tuesday, but they can tell you whether the season will be wet or dry.

Sub-seasonal, 10 to 17 days out, falls in between. The atmosphere has forgotten where it started, but the slow ocean signals are too weak to pin down day-by-day rain. Researchers call this the predictability desert.

Analogy — the billiard ball You can predict exactly where a rolling billiard ball will be one second from now, because you can see its speed and direction. You can also predict that, given the table's tilt, it will eventually settle in the low corner. But predicting precisely where it will be thirty seconds from now — after several unpredictable bounces — is far harder than either. That middle case is sub-seasonal forecasting.

We measured this decay directly for our basin. The global model's rainfall forecast agrees with reality reasonably at short range, and by days 8–17 that agreement has almost vanished. This is not a flaw in our work; it is the physical limit we are working against, and Section 8 returns to what it implies.

2   The Data, and the “Grid” Idea

2.1 What is ECMWF?

ECMWF stands for the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts. It is an intergovernmental research institute that runs one of the best physics-based weather models in the world. When you check a weather app, there is a good chance the numbers ultimately came from a model like this one.

ECMWF also runs a product called S2S, short for “Sub-seasonal to Seasonal.” These are forecasts that reach several weeks ahead instead of several days. That S2S product is the raw material this project tries to improve.

An important point about what we are building We are not building a weather model from scratch. That would require supercomputers and a large physics team. We are building a post-processor — a correction layer that sits on top of ECMWF's physics and fixes its known weaknesses. This is much cheaper, and it is a realistic thing for a small team to do.

2.2 What is a “reforecast”?

To train an AI model you need examples where you know both the question and the answer. So we do not use live forecasts — we use reforecasts: forecasts that ECMWF re-ran for past dates using today's model.

So for, say, 3 July 2009, we have both what the model predicted and what actually happened. That pairing is what allows the AI to learn the difference between the two, which is exactly the correction we want it to make.

2.3 The grid problem: giant pixels versus small ones

This is the single most important concept in Stage 1, so it is worth going slowly.

Every weather model divides the planet into a grid of boxes. Each box gets one number for rainfall, one for temperature, and so on. The model has no idea what happens inside a box.

The ECMWF forecast comes on a grid of 1.5° boxes; our rainfall observations come on a much finer grid of 0.25° boxes. Since 1.5 ÷ 0.25 = 6, each coarse box contains a 6 × 6 block of 36 fine boxes.

2.5 What is an “ensemble,” and why do we only have one?

Because the atmosphere is chaotic, forecasters do not run their model once. They run it many times, each time nudging the starting conditions very slightly. The collection of runs is called an ensemble, and each individual run is a member.

Analogy — ten balls down a hill Ten people roll a ball down the same hill, each releasing it a millimetre apart. If all ten balls end up in the same place, you can be confident about where the next one will go. If they scatter across the whole field, you know the situation is genuinely uncertain. The spread of the ensemble is itself information — it tells you how much to trust the forecast.

The source paper we adapt uses all 10 members. This project has only the single control member. That is a real limitation, honestly stated: it means we cannot use the spread to judge how uncertain any particular forecast is, and instead have to estimate uncertainty from how wrong we have been in the past. Section 7 returns to this.

2.6 The constraint that shaped everything

The inflow measurements end in 2014. The reforecast starts in mid-2004. We can only train and test where both exist — so the usable record is eleven monsoon seasons.

Eleven years is not much. Rainfall forecasts are also only available during the monsoon months (June to September), giving 31 forecast start dates per year, or 339 in total. Everything that follows — especially the choice of how to test the models — is a response to having this little data.

2.7 How do you test a model fairly? (and what “overfitting” means)

This section explains a method rather than a result, but without it none of the numbers later in this document mean anything.

An AI model learns by looking at examples. The danger is that instead of learning the general rule, it simply memorises the examples it was shown.

Analogy — the student who memorised the answer key A student is given last year's exam paper with answers to study. Come the real exam, if the questions are identical, they score 100 %. If the questions are new, they score badly — because they never learned the subject, only the answer key.

This is overfitting. To catch it, you must test on questions the model has genuinely never seen. Testing a model on data it trained on is like grading the student on the paper they memorised: the score is meaningless.

The standard fix is to split your data: train on part, test on the rest. But with only eleven years, holding out one year leaves you with a test based on a single monsoon — and monsoons differ enormously from one another. You might get lucky.

Leave-one-year-out

So instead we do this eleven times over:

  Round 1:  train on 2005..2014          test on 2004
  Round 2:  train on 2004, 2006..2014    test on 2005
  Round 3:  train on 2004,2005,2007..14  test on 2006
     ...                                    ...
  Round 11: train on 2004..2013          test on 2014

  Every year gets a turn as the unseen test year.
  The model is rebuilt FROM SCRATCH each round.

This is called leave-one-year-out cross-validation, abbreviated LOYO. A prediction made by a model that never saw that year is called out-of-fold. Every single number in this document is out-of-fold.

Crucially, it is not only the main model that gets rebuilt each round — the rainfall sharpener, the data scaling factors, and the seasonal averages are all recomputed using only that round's training years. If any one of them had peeked at the test year, the scores would be quietly inflated.

Why this mattered enormously here — a real example Early in the project, the year 2014 was used as the single test year. It later turned out that 2014 was the most predictable of all eleven monsoons. Testing on it alone:
  • overstated the rainfall-sharpening skill by roughly two times;
  • selected the wrong model — a method that looked good on 2014 turned out to fail badly on 2009 when properly tested;
  • and gave the wrong answer about how many days ahead the system is useful.
Every claim in this document that survived proper cross-validation had a plausible-looking single-year version that did not.

2.8 Six mistakes that had to be fixed first

This project inherited an earlier version of the analysis. Before any result could be believed, six errors had to be found and corrected. Each one had made the system look better than it was. They are listed here because understanding them is more instructive than hiding them.

The mistakeWhat it meant, in plain terms
Rainfall totals were never converted to daily amounts The forecast reports rain accumulated since the start. Using that directly means day 10's number includes all the rain from days 1–9. The model was being fed running totals as if they were daily values.
Sea cells were filled with zero Six of twenty-five target boxes were ocean, with no land rainfall. Setting them to zero gave the model six boxes it could always get exactly right — free marks that inflated the score by a quarter.
The data was split randomly Because one calendar day is forecast by several different forecast runs, a random split put the same day in both the training and testing sets. The model had effectively seen the answers.
Scaling was computed before splitting The averages used to normalise the data were calculated over all the data, including the test years. A small leak, but a real one.
The fine rainfall map was thinned to 25 points The target was reduced to the same coarseness as the input, so no downscaling was actually happening at all. The model was being scored on a task it was not performing.
Past-rainfall features were measured from the wrong date “Yesterday's rainfall” was computed relative to the forecast target date, not the date the forecast was issued. At a 17-day lead, “yesterday” was actually 16 days in the future — information no real forecaster could have.
Why an apparently worse result is the better one After these fixes, the headline accuracy score fell from an impressive-looking 0.87 to a modest 0.105. This is not a worse result. It is the first honest one. The 0.87 was measuring leaks and free marks. Anything reported from here on has survived all six corrections.

3   Stage 1 — Sharpening the Rainfall Forecast

3.1 What is “statistical downscaling”?

Statistical downscaling means: learn, from historical data, the relationship between what the coarse model predicted and what actually happened locally — then apply that learned relationship to new forecasts.

It does two jobs at once:

  1. Resolution enhancement — turning one 166 km value into thirty-six 25 km values.
  2. Bias correction — fixing systematic errors, such as “this model always over-predicts rain on the northern slopes in July.”

The alternative is dynamic downscaling: re-running actual atmospheric physics at fine scale. It is more principled but vastly more expensive in computing time. Statistical downscaling is cheap enough to run every day on an ordinary machine, which is what makes it practical.

3.2 What is a neural network, in one paragraph?

A neural network is a large mathematical function with millions of adjustable numbers, called weights. You show it an input and it produces an output. At first the output is nonsense, because the weights are random. You then measure how wrong it was, and nudge every weight slightly in the direction that would have made it less wrong. Repeat this millions of times over thousands of examples, and the weights settle into values that produce good outputs. That nudging process is called training, and the measure of wrongness is called the loss function.

3.3 Why a CNN specifically?

CNN stands for Convolutional Neural Network. It is the architecture designed for grid-shaped data where the spatial neighbourhood matters — the same family of model used for recognising faces in photographs.

The insight is simple but powerful: rain in a valley is not caused only by the atmosphere directly above that valley. It is caused by the wind, moisture and pressure pattern across the whole surrounding region — moisture blowing in from the Bay of Bengal, a low-pressure system sitting to the west. A method that looks only at the local rainfall number is blind to all of that. A CNN can look at the neighbourhood.

3.4 What is a convolution, and what is a “kernel”?

A kernel (also called a filter) is a small grid of learnable weights — here, 3 × 3. It slides across the input, and at each position multiplies each of its nine weights by the nine values underneath, then adds them up. That single number becomes one pixel of the output. Slide it everywhere and you get a whole new grid.

Analogy — the pattern-detector stencil Think of a kernel as a small stencil that lights up when it sees a particular pattern. One kernel might learn to respond to “moist air on the left, dry air on the right” — a moisture front. Another might respond to a sharp pressure gradient. Each kernel becomes a specialist in one atmospheric fingerprint.

The crucial part: nobody programs these stencils by hand. The network discovers which patterns are worth detecting, purely from the training data.

3.5 What goes into the model

For each fine 0.25° box we want to predict, the network is shown a 3 × 3 patch of coarse boxes centred on it — the target's neighbourhood.

Why 3 × 3? The source paper tested 1×1, 3×3, 5×5 and 7×7. Too small (1×1) and you lose the surrounding context, which is the whole advantage a CNN offers. Too large (7×7) and you drag in irrelevant weather from ~580 km away, adding noise and encouraging memorisation. 3×3 was the sweet spot.

At each of those nine boxes the model reads several predictors — different measured quantities:

PredictorWhat it is and why it helps
Total precipitationThe model's own rainfall guess — the thing we are correcting
Surface elevationHow high the land is. The only input that never changes; it tells the network about terrain, and mountains force air upward, which makes rain
Temperature at 2 mWarm air holds more moisture
Wind, east–west and north–southWhich direction moisture is being carried from
Mean sea-level pressureIdentifies low-pressure systems, which bring rain
Cloud coverDirectly related to whether rain is falling
Solar radiation, heat fluxesHow much energy is driving evaporation and convection
An honest gap in this implementation The source paper uses 19 predictors, not our 10. The ones we are missing are all upper-air measurements — wind, humidity, temperature and pressure-surface height taken high in the atmosphere, at three altitudes (roughly 1.5 km, 5.5 km and 12 km up).

Why that matters: weather is three-dimensional. The low level carries the moisture that becomes rain; the middle level shows where storm systems are heading; the top level is the jet stream, which organises everything below it. It is like giving a doctor an X-ray from three angles instead of one. Section 8 explains why obtaining these is the single most valuable next step for this project.

3.6 What is the “vanishing gradient” problem, and what is a ResNet block?

Recall that a network learns by measuring its error and pushing a correction signal backwards through its layers, telling each weight how to adjust. That signal is called the gradient.

The problem: at every layer the signal gets multiplied by numbers usually smaller than one. Multiply repeatedly and it shrinks towards zero. By the time it reaches the earliest layers there is nothing left, and those layers simply stop learning.

Analogy — the whisper down the line Imagine thirty people in a line passing a whispered message backwards. Each person garbles it slightly and speaks a little more softly. By the time it reaches the first person there is nothing but a hiss. Person 1 never finds out what to fix.

A ResNet block (short for “residual network”) solves this with a skip connection: the input to the block is added directly to its output, bypassing the layers entirely.

                 +-------------------------------------------+
                 |            SKIP CONNECTION                |
   input  -------+                                           |
     |                                                       v
     +---> [ Conv 3x3 ] --> [ ELU ] --> [ Conv 3x3 ] ---> (  +  ) ---> output
                                                          ADD

This has two effects. First, the gradient gets a shortcut home — the correction signal travels back along the skip path without being multiplied down. The whisper now has a megaphone line running directly to person 1. Second, the block only has to learn the correction, not the whole mapping. If the best thing it can do is nothing at all, it can simply learn weights near zero and pass the input through untouched. That is why adding depth can never hurt, and why deep networks became trainable at all.

3.7 Why is there an “activation function”?

Between convolution layers sits a small nonlinear function — here, ELU (Exponential Linear Unit). Without one, stacking layers would be pointless: a chain of linear operations collapses mathematically into a single linear operation, and the network could never represent the genuinely nonlinear physics of rainfall. ELU adds the necessary nonlinearity while allowing smooth negative outputs, which keeps gradients flowing rather than letting parts of the network go permanently silent.

3.8 Teaching the network where it is

Here is a subtlety that is easy to miss. Because the same network is applied to every box in the basin, by default it would treat an upland box identically to a valley box — given the same atmospheric inputs, it would predict the same rain. That is physically wrong.

The fix is an embedding layer: the box's grid position is converted into learnable location features, which are merged with the network's output just before the final layers.

Analogy — the postcode It is the difference between a weather app that gives everyone in the country the same forecast, and one that knows your postcode. The embedding is the network's postcode, letting a single shared model learn location-specific behaviour instead of being forced into one global rule.

3.9 The loss function — how we stopped the AI ignoring storms

Recall that the loss function is the measure of wrongness the network tries to minimise. The obvious choice is Mean Squared Error (MSE): take the difference between prediction and truth, square it, average over everything.

But training a rainfall model with plain MSE teaches it a cowardly strategy. Most days are dry or lightly rainy; extreme storms are rare. Predicting something close to the average every single day produces a low average error and gets rewarded.

Analogy — answering “C” to every question A student who answers “C” to every multiple-choice question scores respectably and never has to think. An MSE-trained rainfall model behaves the same way — it hedges towards the middle and never commits to predicting a storm.

For flood forecasting this is a catastrophic failure disguised as good performance. The score looks fine. The model is useless for exactly the days that matter.

The fix: adding a Threat Score

The solution is to add a second term to the loss that specifically punishes missing heavy rain. That term is built from the Threat Score, a standard forecasting measure:

  loss  =  MSE  +  b * (1 - TS)

                    H                 H = HITS         we said heavy, it was heavy
  TS  =  ---------------------        F = FALSE ALARMS we said heavy, it wasn't
           H  +  F  +  M              M = MISSES       it was heavy, we didn't say so

  b = how much we care about extremes relative to everyday accuracy

Reading this intuitively: the MSE term keeps ordinary rainfall accurate; the Threat Score term punishes the model every time it misses a storm. The “always answer C” strategy stops being profitable, and the model is forced to commit.

One mathematical obstacle, and the trick that solves it

Counting hits and misses requires a yes/no decision: was this heavy rain or not? That is a step function — it jumps abruptly from 0 to 1. Step functions have zero slope everywhere, and a network learns by following slopes. There is nothing to follow.

The fix is to replace the hard yes/no with a sigmoid, a smooth S-shaped curve that slides gradually from 0 to 1.

Analogy — the light switch and the dimmer A hard threshold is a light switch: abruptly on or off, telling you nothing about how close you were. A sigmoid is a dimmer — it tells the model “you were nearly there, push a little harder.” That information about near-misses is precisely what learning requires.

One more detail worth noting: what counts as “heavy rain” is defined separately for every box, as that box's own 90th-percentile rainfall. So heavy means something appropriately different in a dry upland box than in a wet valley box.

4   Stage 2 — Turning Rainfall into Inflow

4.1 Why this is genuinely difficult

Sharpened rainfall is only half the chain. We now need to answer: given that this much rain falls over the catchment, how much water passes into the reservoir on each of the next 17 days?

This is called rainfall–runoff modelling, and it is hard for three reasons:

  1. Only a fraction of rain becomes river flow. Some evaporates, some is drunk by plants, some soaks deep into groundwater.
  2. That fraction is not fixed — it depends on how wet the soil already is (the sponge, again).
  3. Water arrives late. Rain falling 300 km upstream takes days to travel down to the dam.

4.2 What is a transformer?

A transformer is a type of neural network built for sequences — ordered lists of things, like words in a sentence or days in a time series. It is the architecture behind modern language models.

Its defining mechanism is called attention. Rather than processing a sequence strictly in order and carrying a running summary, attention lets every output position look directly at every input position and decide, for itself, which ones matter.

Analogy — the researcher and the filing cabinet An older type of model reads a sequence like someone reading a book cover to cover, trying to remember everything in one running summary. By chapter 30 the details of chapter 2 have blurred.

A transformer works like a researcher with the whole filing cabinet open at once. Asked “what will the river do on day 12?”, it can reach directly for the day-12 rainfall file, and also the file on how wet the soil was three weeks ago, without having to pass that information through every intervening day. That direct reach is attention.

4.3 What makes “FutureTST” different?

The model we use is called FutureTST, and the clue is in the name. Most forecasting models look only at the past — the history of the river and the weather — and extrapolate forward.

FutureTST also reads the future: the forecast rainfall sequence over the days it is predicting.

This sounds like cheating, but it is not. We genuinely have a rainfall forecast for the next 17 days — that is what Stage 1 produced. What we do not know is how much of that rain will reach the dam, and when. So the model is given the future weather and asked to work out the future river.

Why this is the right design Attention can route the fact “40 mm of rain is expected on day 9” directly into the day-9 river prediction. An older model would have to carry that fact forward through every day from 1 to 9, degrading it along the way. This is precisely why the model can stay useful at long lead times — and Section 6 shows measurable evidence that it does.

The two halves have names worth knowing. The encoder reads the input and builds an internal summary. The decoder produces the output, and cross-attention is the mechanism by which each of its 17 questions reaches back into the encoder's summary.

Analogy — the briefing and the questions The encoder is an analyst who reads every weather report and writes a detailed briefing. The decoder is seventeen different officials, each asking one question: “what happens on my day?” Cross-attention is each official being allowed to read the whole briefing and pick out the parts relevant to their own day — rather than being handed a summary of a summary passed down a chain.

4.6 Two mistakes we made, and what fixing them taught us

The first version of Stage 2 performed poorly, and the conclusion drawn was “there is not enough data for a transformer to work here.” That conclusion was wrong, and discovering why was one of the most valuable parts of the project.

What we did at firstWhat the paper actually does
Training

The lesson, stated plainly “Not enough data” was not a fact about the Mahanadi basin. It was a fact about how we had set up the problem. We had created our own data shortage by only training on forecast days, and we had removed the very mechanism the paper credits for its performance. Both errors were ours. Fixing them changed the result completely.

4.7 A subtle problem: trained on truth, used on forecasts

Following the source paper, the model is trained on measured weather — what actually fell. This gives us far more training examples, which is exactly what a data-starved model needs.

But there is a catch, and it is the central difficulty of the whole project.

During training, the future rainfall the model sees is true. So it learns to trust that information completely. In real use, the future rainfall is a forecast, and at day 15 that forecast is only weakly related to reality. The model does not know this. It reads a wrong rainfall number with total confidence and converts it into a wrong river prediction.

Analogy — the trainee who only ever saw perfect data Imagine training a junior engineer using instruments that are always perfectly calibrated. They learn, sensibly, to trust the dial completely. Now send them to a field site where the day-15 gauge is known to be badly unreliable. They will keep trusting the dial — and confidently make bad decisions. The problem is not their competence. It is that nobody taught them which instruments to doubt.

Two remedies are tested in this project:

  • Fine-tuning — after training on measured weather, continue training briefly on the smaller set of real forecast examples, at a very low learning rate. This lets the model adjust how much it leans on its rainfall input without destroying the rainfall–runoff relationship it already learned. (Fine-tuning in general means: take an already-trained model and adapt it to a related task with a small amount of extra training.)
  • Lead-dependent noise training — deliberately corrupt the training rainfall by exactly as much as the real forecast is wrong at each day, so the model learns from the start to trust day 2 and doubt day 15.

Section 6 reports how well each works.

5   How Do We Know If It Works?

Before any results, we need to understand the scoring rules. A number like “NSE +0.34” means nothing until you know what it is measuring and what counts as good.

5.1 The most important one: NSE

NSE stands for Nash–Sutcliffe Efficiency, and it is the standard score in hydrology. It answers one question:

What NSE actually asks “Is this model better than just predicting the seasonal average every single day?”

That lazy strategy — ignore all information and predict the long-term average — is the benchmark. NSE measures how much better you are than it.
NSE valueWhat it means
1.0Perfect. Every prediction exactly right.
0.7Very good for a river forecast.
0.5Generally regarded as satisfactory for daily flow.
0.0Exactly as good as predicting the average — i.e. worthless.
below 0Worse than predicting the average. You would have been better off not building the model.

NSE can go arbitrarily negative, which is why you occasionally see alarming numbers like −0.87 in cross-validation results.

Why NSE is a harsh judge at long lead times NSE punishes errors in the timing of peaks very heavily. Suppose the model correctly predicts a large flood, but places it on Tuesday when it actually arrived on Thursday. NSE penalises this twice — once for predicting a flood on a quiet day, and once for missing the flood on the day it happened. The model understood the situation and got the total volume of water right, and NSE still scores it terribly.

This is worth remembering when reading the long-lead results. It is also why the authors of Paper 1, facing the same issue, additionally report the error in total water volume — which for a reservoir operator is arguably the more useful quantity anyway.

5.2 R² — used for the rainfall stage

R² ("R squared") measures the fraction of the variation in the truth that the model successfully explains. 1.0 is perfect; 0 means the model explains nothing; negative means it is actively worse than a flat guess.

An R² of about 0.10 for daily rainfall in a single 25 km box sounds low, and it is. But the honest context is that raw ECMWF scores −0.136 on the same task, and Section 8 explains why roughly 0.1 is close to the physical ceiling given the information available.

5.3 Correlation

Correlation measures whether two things rise and fall together, ignoring whether the magnitudes match. It runs from −1 to +1. A correlation of 0.6 between forecast and observed catchment rainfall means the forecast reliably knows when wet spells are coming, even if it gets the exact amounts wrong.

5.4 KGE

KGE (Kling–Gupta Efficiency) is a companion to NSE that separates skill into three parts: getting the timing right, getting the variability right, and getting the average level right. It is quoted alongside NSE because a model can score well on one and badly on another, and knowing which failed tells you what to fix.

5.5 Two phrases you will see repeatedly

PhraseMeaning
Out-of-foldA prediction made by a model that never saw that year during training. Everything reported here is out-of-fold. See §2.7.
Lead time / lead dayHow far ahead the prediction is. “Lead 12” means we are predicting 12 days after the forecast was issued.

There is also a helpful effect when we average over the catchment. Individual box errors are largely independent, so they partly cancel. Correlation with reality for catchment-average rainfall is 0.59 after sharpening, against 0.41 raw — much healthier than the per-box figure suggests.

A cautionary tale about testing On the single year 2014, the simpler method scored 0.142 and the CNN 0.118 — the simpler method looked better. Tested properly across all eleven years, the ranking reverses decisively. Had we trusted the single-year test, we would have shipped the wrong model. This is the concrete cost of the shortcut described in §2.7.

Now split those results by how far ahead we are predicting.

Read the first column downwards — this is the proof the design works With measured weather, the model gets better as it looks further ahead: +0.538, +0.545, +0.587, +0.615. Every other model in this project gets worse with distance.

Why does this prove something? A model that is better at 17 days than at 3 days cannot be relying on yesterday's flow — that information is useless at 17 days. It can only be reading the future rainfall sequence. This is exactly what cross-attention (§4.4) was supposed to enable, and it is direct evidence that the mechanism is doing its job.
Now read the second column — this is the whole problem Swap measured weather for forecast weather and +0.615 becomes −0.229. The rise turns into a collapse.

The reason is exactly the trainee-with-perfect-instruments problem from §4.7. Precisely where the model's greatest strength lies — its ability to read future rainfall directly — it is most exposed when that rainfall is wrong. Its strength and its vulnerability are the same mechanism.

Fine-tuning repairs much of the damage, lifting 13–17 days from −0.229 back to a positive +0.113. It cannot repair all of it, because no amount of clever training can invent rainfall information that was never in the forecast.

6.4 Something that was tried and did not work

A standard technique called quantile mapping reshapes a forecast so its overall statistics match observed reality — if the forecast never predicts more than 30 mm but reality sometimes reaches 60 mm, quantile mapping stretches the forecast to match.

It was implemented and tested. It made things worse (+0.136 against +0.288 for fine-tuning alone).

Analogy — turning up the volume on static If a radio signal is mostly noise, turning up the volume does not help you hear the music. It just gives you louder noise.

Quantile mapping restores the size of extreme rainfall values without knowing when they should occur. Applied to a forecast that is only weakly correlated with reality, it adds amplitude without adding information — producing confident predictions of storms on the wrong days. Negative results like this are worth reporting: they save the next person the effort.

6.5 Saying how confident we are

A single number ("8,400 cumecs on Thursday") is less useful to an operator than a range ("between 5,100 and 12,600, with 80 % confidence"). We produce these using conformal prediction, a method that builds ranges with a guaranteed hit rate by measuring how wrong the model has been on data it did not train on.

The test is simple: if we claim 80 % confidence, does the truth actually land inside the range 80 % of the time?

We claimCalibrated on 2 yearsCalibrated on all 11
89 % confidence86.0 %89.6 %
80 % confidence69.2 %81.9 %
50 % confidence46.9 %50.9 %

The middle column is dangerous: claiming 80 % confidence while being right only 69 % of the time means an operator who trusts your range gets caught out far more often than they expect. Using all eleven years to calibrate fixes it almost exactly. The lesson was that the ranges were not the wrong shape — we simply had not used enough years to measure them.

7   What Does Not Work, and Why

A trustworthy report states its own boundaries before anyone has to ask. These are ours.

7.1 The system underestimates the biggest floods

This is the most serious limitation, and it must not be buried.

The largest flood in 2014 reached 22,308 cumecs. Our forecast, seven days ahead, said 6,274 cumecs — about 28 % of what actually arrived. Fed into a release calculation, that would have sized the operation for a flood roughly 3.6 times too small, and the reservoir would have overtopped uncontrollably.

Stated as plainly as possible This system is not currently trustworthy for extreme flood decisions. It is useful for routine operations and moderate flows. A separate check confirms this independently: the system's skill at predicting whether flow will exceed the most extreme threshold is slightly negative, meaning it adds nothing over guessing at that level.

Why does this happen? It is the “answering C” problem from §3.9, resurfacing further down the chain. Models trained to minimise average error naturally retreat towards moderate predictions when they are uncertain — and at seven days ahead, they are uncertain. The threat-score loss fights this in Stage 1, but it does not eliminate it.

7.2 We only have one forecast, not ten

As explained in §2.5, the proper input is a 10-member ensemble whose spread indicates confidence. We have a single member. So our uncertainty ranges are built from past errors, and cannot recognise that this particular forecast is an unusually uncertain one. Obtaining the full ensemble is the single most valuable improvement available to this project.

7.3 Part of the catchment is invisible to us

The forecast data was downloaded over a box that turns out to be slightly too small. The western headwaters of the catchment fall outside it entirely, and about half of the fine boxes sit near the edge, where the model's 3×3 neighbourhood is partly padding rather than real data. Rain falling in those western headwaters does reach the dam — we simply cannot see it coming.

7.4 Eleven monsoons is not many

The inflow record ends in 2014, so no amount of effort produces more training years. This has a specific consequence, verified directly: with its safety limits switched off, the transformer achieves a near-perfect score on its training data. That is not a good sign — it means it has enough capacity to memorise the examples rather than learn the rule. Making the model bigger is the one change guaranteed not to help.

7.5 The reservoir-operation part is unvalidated

The project includes a module that converts an inflow forecast into a suggested release. It has never been checked against reality, because no record of Hirakud's actual storage levels or releases was available. The reservoir constants it uses are published design values, not the operating rules currently in force. It is a demonstration, not an operational tool.

7.6 We forecast 17 days, not 30

The original plan aimed for 30 days. The reforecast data actually available reaches only 17. This is a data limitation, not a modelling choice.

8   Three Findings Worth Remembering

8.1 The rainfall score is at a physical ceiling, not a modelling failure

An R² of about 0.1 for daily rainfall in a 25 km box looks disappointing. Seven different approaches were tried to improve it — different network designs, different loss functions, extra features, a completely different type of model — and all seven landed in the same place.

Two measurements explain why:

  1. If you give a model perfect knowledge of the coarse-scale weather — cheating completely — it scores about 0.71, not 1.0. So roughly 29 % of the fine detail simply does not exist at the coarse scale. No model can recover it, because the information was never there.
  2. ECMWF's own rainfall forecast becomes almost uncorrelated with reality beyond about a week. Most of the remaining variation is not in the input either.
Analogy — enhancing a blurry photograph Films love the moment where someone says “enhance!” and a blurred face becomes sharp. In reality, if the original photograph never captured those details, no software can invent them — it can only guess plausibly. Our sharpener is doing genuinely well at guessing plausibly. It cannot recover what the camera never recorded.
The practical consequence Further work on model design is not worthwhile; the ceiling has been established from several directions. Progress requires more input information — the 10-member ensemble, the missing upper-air predictors (§3.5), and a wider download area. That is a data-acquisition task, not a machine-learning one.

8.2 The rainfall forecast is the bottleneck — and we proved it the hard way

The evidence is the gap in §6.2: +0.577 with measured weather versus +0.103 with forecast weather. That gap sits upstream of every decision about the river model.

It would have been easy to assert this. Instead it was tested, by building a genuine physics-based hydrological model — the kind that simulates soil layers, evaporation, snowmelt and water routing from real physical equations — and asking whether it did better.

ModelFed measured rainfallFed forecast rainfall
Physics-based model0.9100.332
Simple statistical model0.7610.435

Read that carefully. The physics-based model is genuinely better at turning rain into runoff — 0.910 against 0.761. And it loses when the rain is a forecast.

Why physics does not rescue you here A physical model carries an internal state — how much water is in the soil right now — and updates it every day using the rainfall it is given. If that rainfall is wrong, the error does not just affect today; it is absorbed into the state and contaminates every following day.

A simpler statistical model has no such memory to poison. So physical models are more sensitive to rainfall error, not less. This is the opposite of most people's intuition, and it is why the physics-based model, though built and fully calibrated, is used here as a scientific check rather than as part of the operational forecast.
Analogy — the camera Upgrading from a phone camera to a professional lens transforms your photographs — provided the camera body can resolve the detail. Bolt the same lens onto a twenty-year-old point-and-shoot and the pictures barely change.

The rainfall forecast is the lens. This project's finding is that the lens is the limit, and no change of camera body fixes it.

8.3 Both times we blamed the data, the fault was ours

Twice in this project a disappointing result was attributed to something external, and twice that was wrong.

  • “The transformer fails because there is not enough data” — in fact we had created the shortage ourselves by training on only 341 days instead of 4,302 (§4.6).
  • “The comparison shows transformers are unsuited to this basin” — in fact the competing model was being handed carefully hand-built summary features while the transformer had to work them out for itself. Given the same information, the gap largely closed.
The general lesson, useful well beyond hydrology A fair comparison between two models requires giving them the same information, not merely the same test rows. And an apparent limit of the data is very often a limit of the experimental setup. In both cases here, the honest explanation was less flattering than the original one — and considerably more useful.
